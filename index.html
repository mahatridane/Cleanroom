01 / Introduction

What is a semiconductor wafer?

Go to the lab →

Wafer

A wafer is a thin disc of semiconductor material, such as silicon, silicon carbide, or gallium arsenide, on which many chips are made.

One wafer → many chips

Chip (die)

A chip (die) is a small piece of the wafer containing an electronic circuit. Each square represents one die.

Die area → mm²

Defect

A defect is a flaw that can stop a chip from working. In this model, one killer defect makes a die fail.

Defect density → killer defects/cm²

Yield

Yield is the fraction of complete dies that work.

Working dies ÷ complete dies

05 / FAQs

FAQs

Why are the two yield values different?

“Yield on this wafer” counts the working dies in the displayed sample. “Expected yield” predicts the average across many wafers. Random defect locations make individual samples differ. Guided missions use the prediction.

Does wafer diameter affect yield?

At the same die area and defect density, predicted wafer yield stays the same. A larger wafer fits more complete dies, so it produces more usable chips on average. A single simulated wafer can still have a different measured yield.

Which wafer sizes are used, and why?

300 mm (about 12 inches) is widely used for high-volume logic and memory. 200 mm (about 8 inches) remains important for power, analog and sensor chips. Smaller formats, including 150 mm, are also used.

A larger wafer carries more chips per processing step. But a fab must balance that benefit against equipment cost, demand and process compatibility. Existing tools and suppliers make established sizes economical.

Sources: SEMI · 300 mm · SEMI · 200 mm · WaferPro · available sizes

Why aren't 18-inch wafers the standard?

450 mm wafers (roughly 18 inches) can be made. The obstacle is economical mass production. Moving to that size requires new or redesigned processing, inspection and handling equipment, plus development to keep the process uniform over a larger area.

Those costs need enough customers and production volume to pay off. The industry has continued investing in 300 mm production instead of adopting 450 mm as the standard. Bigger is useful only when the whole factory can make it cost-effective.

Sources: G450C · equipment development · ASML · paused 450 mm development · SEMI · current 300 mm investment

Why use the Poisson model?

It connects two inputs—defect density and die area—to the chance of a die having no killer defects. If defects occur independently at a uniform average density, the average defects per die is D × A, and the chance of zero is e−D·A.

This makes the relationship easy to explore. Real manufacturing may need models that account for clustered defects and other failures; Poisson is a useful starting point, not a universal factory forecast.

Source: NIST · Poisson distribution

Are all defects caused by dust?

No. Particles can cause defects, but so can unwanted impurities, patterning errors and other process problems. The enlarged dots represent killer defects: flaws that make a die fail. Harmless imperfections are not counted in this model.

Why does die size matter?

A larger die covers more area, so it is more likely to contain a killer defect. Fewer large dies also fit on the wafer. At fixed defect density, smaller dies have higher predicted yield.

What happens at the edge?

Only dies that fit completely inside the wafer count. Incomplete edge pieces are excluded from both the working-chip count and the yield fraction.

Behind the wafer

Yield and usable chips

Wafer yield is good complete dies ÷ all complete dies. Incomplete edge pieces do not enter this fraction. A higher yield percentage does not always mean more usable chips.

Y = e−DA · Ngood = NfitY

D is killer defects per cm². A is die area in cm²: divide mm² by 100. The product DA is the average number of defects per die. In a Poisson model, e−DA is the probability of zero defects.

Reading the map

Each enlarged dot is a killer defect. One or more hits make a die fail. Several hits in one die still lose only one chip, so do not subtract the number of defects from the die count.

Changing area keeps defect locations fixed. Increasing density adds defects to the same pattern. “Run a new wafer” samples a new pattern. A simulated wafer can differ from the expected average. Fractional expected chip counts are averages, not pieces of chips. The fraction under the wafer uses the actual working and complete die counts from that displayed simulation; the expected metrics show the model averages.

The classroom game

Numerical comparisons use the expected result, not a random wafer. Concept questions cover the same model and assumptions. Before the reveal, the neutral purple grid shows die size and capacity. The result ring shows expected yield; the numeric result follows the question. Both wafers use the same physical drawing scale within a round.

Assumptions

  • Uniform, independent killer defects. Real defects can cluster; many imperfections are harmless. Dopants are not the defects modeled here.
  • Square dies on a fixed grid crossing the wafer centre. Only dies with all four corners inside the circle count. Placement is not optimized; cutting lanes and edge exclusion are omitted.
  • This is defect-limited wafer yield. It excludes other electrical failures, repair, redundancy, packaging and testing losses.

Why it matters

For a fixed wafer cost, fewer usable chips means a higher fabrication cost per good chip. Reducing defect density improves the usable fraction.
